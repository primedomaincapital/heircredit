What Inheritance-Advance Lending Actually Costs: The Fee Schedule Behind the Wait

An inheritance advance typically costs 10 to 40 percent of the amount you receive — and the fastest probate is the most expensive money you will ever borrow. The fee schedule is the product.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

A woman in Los Angeles is offered $15,000 today against her share of her mother's estate. In exchange she signs away $20,000 of her inheritance when the estate distributes. The paperwork calls it an assignment, not a loan. There is no interest rate on the page, no monthly payment, no credit check. The cost is $5,000 — 25 percent of the $20,000 she assigns, or 33 percent of the $15,000 she actually receives — and whether that is a fair price or an expensive one depends entirely on a number that appears nowhere in the agreement: how long the estate takes to close.

The fee schedule behind the wait is priced off the timeline, and the industry's own published ranges make that plain. For a probate expected to take three to six months, fees typically run 10 to 20 percent of the amount received. For six to twelve months, 15 to 30 percent. For twelve to twenty-four months, 25 to 40 percent. For contested cases running past two years, 35 to 50 percent or more. Most funding companies advance between 30 and 70 percent of the expected inheritance — some put the range at 20 to 50 percent — and most require the expected inheritance to meet a minimum of about $15,000, with a minimum advance of $3,000 to $5,000. On a $50,000 advance, the fee runs anywhere from $5,000 to $20,000, meaning the heir repays $55,000 to $70,000 from the inheritance when the estate closes. The range is wide because the risk is wide: the longer and more uncertain the probate, the more the company charges to wait for it.

Two pricing models sit behind those ranges, and the difference between them decides whether the fee grows while you wait. Under a fixed-fee arrangement, the company charges a flat dollar amount regardless of how long the estate takes to close: receive $10,000 today by assigning $13,500, and the cost is $3,500 whether probate wraps up in four months or two years. Under a tiered discount model, the fee increases at set intervals, often every six months — the lowest rate if the estate settles within the first 180 days, a higher rate after that, and higher still after a year. The tiered model is the one to read carefully, because it charges you more for the company's own delay. A fixed fee rewards patience. A tiered fee punishes it.

The annualised math is what turns a reasonable-sounding discount into an expensive loan. Take the Los Angeles example: $15,000 received, $20,000 assigned, a $5,000 cost. If the estate distributes in eight months, the heir paid 33 percent for eight months of liquidity — roughly a 50 percent annualised rate. If the estate distributes in four months, that same $5,000 works out to an annualised rate near 100 percent. The twist is the one the marketing never mentions: the faster the estate closes, the worse the deal was, because the fixed discount does not shrink when you only needed the money briefly. These are not hypothetical numbers. Horton and Chandrasekher's Probate Lending study, published in the Yale Law Journal in 2016, examined 594 California probate files and found companies had advanced $808,500 in exchange for $1,378,786 in inheritance rights, with an average repayment time of 373 days and a mean annualised markup of about 69 percent. A San Francisco follow-up measured an average effective annual rate around 127 percent.

The reason no annual rate appears on the paperwork is the structure. An inheritance advance is a sale of part of the inheritance interest, not a loan, so no interest rate is disclosed and usury rules do not apply. There are no monthly payments, no interest accruing, and no impact on the heir's credit. The advance is non-recourse: the funding company collects directly from the estate, and if the estate cannot pay the full amount, the heir owes nothing. That structure is the whole pitch, and it is also the whole problem — because the only way to evaluate the offer is to do the loan math yourself, and the companies have no obligation to do it for you. California's answer is court oversight: Probate Code section 11604.5 requires these agreements to be filed with the court and lets a judge refuse ones that are grossly unreasonable, and the Probate Code contains a cheaper alternative — a partial distribution or an advance from the estate itself — that the companies never mention. Inheritances are generally not treated as taxable income for the beneficiary under federal law, and the federal estate tax exemption is $15,000,000 for 2026, so for almost every family the tax question is not the binding one. The price question is.

Comparing offers in practice is where most heirs leave money on the table, because the fee is quoted as a percentage and the percentages are not comparable without the timeline. Get two or three quotes, and ask each company for the fee in dollars, not percent, on the same advance amount and the same expected closing date. Ask whether the company charges application or processing fees — most say they charge nothing unless and until the heir receives the advance, and any company that charges upfront is telling you something about its business. Ask what happens if probate runs long: a fixed fee stays flat, a tiered fee steps up, and the difference on a $25,000 advance can be several thousand dollars. And watch the small advances most carefully. A $3,000 advance at a 40 percent fee costs $1,200 — the smallest advances carry the highest percentages, because the company's fixed costs of underwriting the estate do not shrink with the size of the deal. The heir who borrows the least pays the highest rate, which is the opposite of how lending is supposed to work.

Here is the counterargument, and it deserves a fair hearing. The fee is the price of non-recourse, no-credit-check, no-monthly-payment liquidity, and the company bears the risk that the estate pays less than expected, pays late, or does not pay at all. A credit card at 25 percent or more demands monthly payments and a credit check, and a bank loan demands both plus collateral — none of which an heir waiting on probate can reliably offer. The advance puts cash in hand in days, with no payment due until the estate closes, and if the estate turns out to be insolvent, the heir walks away. For a family facing foreclosure, a medical bill, or a funeral cost, that structure has real value, and the 69 percent average markup in the Yale study is the average across all files — including the contested and the delayed, where the company's risk was highest. The court backstop in California exists, and judges do refuse the worst deals.

The implication is practical, and it fits on an index card. Before signing, do the loan math yourself: divide the cost by the amount you actually receive, then annualise it over the expected wait. Compare offers, because the 10-to-40-percent range is wide enough that two companies can quote the same heir prices thousands of dollars apart. Ask whether the fee is fixed or tiered, and what happens if probate runs past the first six months. And check the probate timeline first, because the fee is priced off it — a six-month wait in Texas and an eighteen-month wait in California are not the same product at the same price, and the cheapest advance is the one you do not take when the estate is about to close. The fee schedule is the product. Read it before you buy it.

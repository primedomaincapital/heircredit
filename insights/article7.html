The Heir Who Can't Wait: When an Advance Is the Right Call — and When It Isn't

An inheritance advance is the right call when the money protects something worth more than the fee — a home, a business, a medical bill. It is the wrong call when the wait is short, the need is discretionary, or a cheaper door is open.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The mortgage on the family house is ninety days past due, and the estate that would pay it is eighteen months from closing. The heir has two choices: find $15,000 now, or lose the house the inheritance was supposed to include. That is the situation an inheritance advance was built for — not the abstract one, not the marketing one, the one where the money protects something worth more than the fee. The advance is a tool with a price, and like any tool it is right for some jobs and wrong for others. The difference is not the fee schedule. It is the reason for the money.

The right call starts with the arithmetic of protection. An advance is defensible when the money prevents a loss larger than the fee: a foreclosure that would sell the house below market, a small business that misses payroll and loses its staff, a medical bill that goes to collections, or the estate's own expenses — property tax, insurance, maintenance — that protect the value of what the heir is waiting to receive. The numbers make the case plainly. On a $50,000 inheritance, a $15,000 advance at a 25 percent fee costs $3,750. A foreclosure costs the house. A lapsed insurance policy on an empty house costs whatever the next storm does. The fee is expensive, and the alternative is worse. The advance also makes sense when there is no other credit available: no income to service a loan, no home equity to borrow against, no credit score a bank will accept. The advance requires none of those things — no credit check, no monthly payments, no collateral beyond the inheritance itself — and for an heir with no income, that is not a marketing line. It is the only door open.

The wrong call is easier to recognise, and it is the more common one. The advance is the wrong call when the wait is short. The fixed discount does not shrink because you only needed the money briefly: a $5,000 fee on a $15,000 advance that the estate repays in four months is an annualised rate near 100 percent, and no discretionary purchase is worth that. It is the wrong call when the need is discretionary — a car, a holiday, a renovation — because the fee buys nothing that time would not deliver for free. It is the wrong call when the estate is contested or insolvent, because the fee climbs to 35 to 50 percent or more on the longest timelines, and the company may not pay at all if the inheritance is uncertain. And it is the wrong call when the heir has not checked the will, because an advance signed against a share that a contest later reduces is a debt against money that never arrives — non-recourse protects the heir from owing the company, but it does not protect the heir from having sold a share they did not have.

The cheapest door is the one the advance companies never mention, and it is worth checking before any other. In California, the Probate Code contains a cheaper alternative to the commercial advance — a partial distribution or an advance from the estate itself, approved by the court — and section 11604.5 requires commercial agreements to be filed with the court, where a judge can refuse ones that are grossly unreasonable. An executor can ask the court for a preliminary distribution. A family loan, even at an interest rate a bank would charge, costs a fraction of a 25 percent discount. A home-equity line, a zero-percent credit card for a short wait, or a short-term loan secured against other assets all beat the advance's annualised cost in most cases — the Yale study's mean annualised markup of about 69 percent, and the San Francisco follow-up's average effective rate around 127 percent, are the numbers to beat, and most forms of ordinary credit beat them easily. The advance is the most expensive money available, and it should be the last resort, not the first call.

The first conversation should not be with an advance company. It should be with the executor. The executor controls the estate's money, and the executor can ask the court for a preliminary distribution — a partial payment to the heirs before the estate closes — which costs the heir nothing but the court's time. Many executors do not know this is available, or do not offer it unprompted, and the heir who asks is often the heir who gets paid first. The executor can also tell the heir something the advance company cannot: whether the will is likely to be contested, whether the estate holds debts the heir does not know about, and whether the share the heir is about to sell is the share they will actually receive. An advance signed before the will is admitted to probate is a bet on an outcome the heir has not verified, and the non-recourse clause protects the heir from owing the company — it does not protect the heir from having sold a share that was never theirs.

Here is the counterargument, and it is the one the industry makes for itself. The advance is not a loan, and comparing it to a loan misses the point. It is non-recourse: if the estate cannot pay the full amount, the heir owes nothing, and no credit card or home-equity line offers that. It requires no credit check, no income verification, and no monthly payments, which means it serves people the credit system has already turned away. And it is fast — cash in days, not the weeks a bank takes — which is the whole point when the foreclosure notice has a date on it. The Yale study's 69 percent average is the average across all files, including the contested and the delayed, where the company's risk was highest; a straightforward estate with a clear will and a solvent executor is not the average file. The companies bear real risk, and the fee is the price of it.

There is truth in that, and it is worth saying plainly, because the critics' case can sound as if no advance is ever justified. But the counterargument has a limit, and the limit is the one this article keeps returning to: the fee does not shrink with the wait, the risk does not fall with the price, and the company has no obligation to tell the heir that a cheaper door is open. The non-recourse protection is real, but it protects the heir from the worst case, not from the bad deal. The speed is real, but speed is only worth paying for when the alternative is a loss. The test is not whether the advance is fair in the abstract. It is whether the money protects something worth more than the fee, and whether no cheaper door is open.

The decision, reduced to a sequence, is this. First, name the need: is it protecting an asset, paying a bill that cannot wait, or funding something discretionary? Second, name the wait: how long until the estate distributes, in this state, in this county, on this file? Third, check the cheaper doors: a partial distribution from the estate, a family loan, a credit card for a short wait, a home-equity line. Fourth, if no cheaper door is open and the need is real, do the loan math on the advance — the cost divided by the amount received, annualised over the expected wait — and compare offers, because the 10-to-40-percent range is wide enough that two companies can quote the same heir prices thousands of dollars apart. The advance is the right call when the money protects something worth more than the fee and no cheaper door is open. Every other time, the cheapest advance is the one you do not take.

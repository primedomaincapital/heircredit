Twenty Months Is the Product

Probate takes twenty months on average. That wait is the entire reason the inheritance-advance business exists.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 6, 2026 · 7 min read

She had the will, the death certificate, and a mortgage payment due in eleven days. The estate was worth a little over $400,000, her share was clear, and nobody was fighting. She still could not touch a dollar of it. The court had its own calendar, the creditors had their own window, and the house — the largest asset in the estate — needed months to sell. That gap, between money that is legally yours and money you can actually spend, is where a whole industry lives.

The gap is longer than almost anyone expects. Trust & Will, the digital estate-planning platform, surveyed 1,000 Americans in March 2024 for its State of Probate study and found the average probate case runs about 20 months from filing to final distribution. Only 2% of respondents guessed that figure. Fifteen percent thought the process took one to three months; another 15% guessed four to six. More than a third admitted they had no idea at all. The public's mental model of probate is a few weeks of paperwork. The reality is closer to two years.

The delay is not laziness. It is built into the procedure. Every probate case has a creditor claim period written into state law, and that period sets a floor no executor can go under: three months in Florida, four months in California, seven months in New York. Add the time to open the case, inventory the assets, sell or value the real estate, settle debts, file taxes, and get a final accounting approved, and the months stack up. U.S. Bank's trust and probate specialist, Jonathan Longfield, puts the general estimate at about 18 months, with most courts expecting completion in roughly that window — and a small, simple, uncontested estate still needing four to six months in the faster states. Real estate inside the estate adds an estimated four to eight months on its own, for appraisals, title work, and court-confirmed sales.

Waiting is also expensive. Trust & Will's study puts the average total cost of probate at 3% to 7% of the estate's gross value. Settled Estate, which tracks filing fees across 3,223 counties, gives a similar planning range of 3% to 8%. On a $500,000 estate, that is $15,000 to $40,000 gone before a single heir is paid — attorney fees, executor compensation, court costs, appraisals, and bonds. The costs are paid from the estate, so the heir never writes the check. The heir simply receives less, later.

That combination — a long wait, a certain cost, and a certain outcome — is exactly what an inheritance advance is priced against. The structure is simple, and it is not a loan. The heir sells a portion of a future distribution to a funding company at a discount. The company files the assignment with the probate court, waits with the heir, and collects its share directly from the estate when it distributes. There is no credit check, no monthly payment, and no personal liability: if the estate comes up short, the company absorbs the loss, because the transaction is non-recourse. The oldest firm in the field, Inheritance Funding Company, has operated since 1992, holds a Better Business Bureau A+ rating dating to 1995, and says it has advanced more than $200 million to heirs. Its advances run from $5,000 to $350,000, up to 40% of the expected inheritance, for heirs inheriting at least $10,000. TriMark Legal Funding, in the business since 2003, says it has funded more than $105 million and can pay heirs in as little as 24 hours. InheritCashNow, a newer entrant, reports helping more than 1,000 families across all 50 states, with funds typically wired within 24 to 48 hours of approval.

The price of speed is the discount, and the discount is steep. Fee guides put the typical cost at 10% to 40% of the amount received, with most transactions landing between 20% and 40%. The fee rises with the expected wait: InheritCashNow's published bands run 10% to 15% for a three-to-six-month probate, 15% to 25% for six to twelve months, 20% to 30% for twelve to eighteen, and 25% to 40% beyond eighteen months. A $10,000 advance can mean assigning $13,000 to $14,000 of a future inheritance. A $30,000 advance on a $100,000 share can cost $10,000 to $15,000. Most firms cap the advance at 30% to 35% of the heir's share, precisely because the discount compounds against the heir's remaining distribution.

Here is the honest counterweight: for an heir who can wait, waiting is free, and the advance is a bad deal on pure arithmetic. A 30% discount for money that would arrive in twenty months anyway is a punishing rate of return in the funder's favor. Consumer guides are blunt about this, and they should be. The advance makes sense only when the heir cannot wait — a mortgage, a medical bill, a business payroll, a house in the estate that needs carrying costs paid while it sits unsold. The industry's own pricing concedes the point: the fee is a function of time, and time is the one thing probate never gives back. There is also a regulatory wrinkle worth naming. Because advances are structured as purchases of a future interest rather than loans, they sit outside the Truth in Lending Act and most state usury laws. That is why the non-recourse term and the written dollar figure matter so much: the heir's protection is the contract, not the lending code.

The implication for anyone building in this market is to sell the wait, not the money. The money is a commodity — every funder wires it in a day or two. The wait is the product: twenty months, a creditor window, a house that will not sell itself, and a family that needs cash before the court is finished. A brand that names the heir and the credit in the same breath is a brand that can own that wait. The firms that win will be the ones whose pricing is transparent enough to survive a grieving family's second look, and whose name tells the heir, before a single form is filled in, that the money can arrive before the estate does.

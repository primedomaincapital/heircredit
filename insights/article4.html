Three Numbers for the Same Fortune

Cerulli says $124 trillion. UBS says $83.5 trillion. Visa says $36 trillion. The transfer is real; the price tag depends on what you count — and the gap is where heirs get hurt.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Ask three of the most respected research teams in finance how much wealth is about to change hands in America, and you get three answers that do not look like they describe the same event. Cerulli Associates says $124 trillion through 2048. UBS says $83.5 trillion over the next two decades. Visa’s economic research team, publishing in July 2026, says $36 trillion will actually reach Gen X and millennial heirs over twenty years. The gap between the highest and the lowest is nearly $90 trillion — roughly three times the annual output of the American economy, supposedly describing the same handover.

Start with the number everyone quotes. Cerulli’s original estimate, published in 2021, was $84.4 trillion through 2045, with $72.6 trillion going to heirs and $11.9 trillion to charity. Its 2024 refresh raised the total to $124 trillion through 2048, with $105 trillion to heirs and $18 trillion to charity. The revision was driven by asset growth: equities rose 27% and real estate 39% between 2020 and 2023. The money is concentrated, not spread evenly — more than $62 trillion, over half the total, comes from the roughly 2% of households that are high-net-worth or ultra-high-net-worth. And much of it stops along the way: Cerulli projects $54 trillion passing to spouses before it ever reaches the next generation, with nearly $40 trillion of that going to widowed women in the Boomer and older cohorts. Millennials inherit the most over the full horizon, about $46 trillion. Nearly half of inheritors — 47% — say they will not keep their benefactor’s advisor.

Visa’s $36 trillion is not a different guess about how rich boomers are. Every one of these institutions agrees on the starting point: boomers hold somewhere around $88 trillion to $93 trillion in total net worth in the United States alone. The disagreement is about what happens to that pile between now and the moment it would theoretically pass to heirs. Visa subtracts the spending, the healthcare, the longevity, and the taxes — the money boomers will use themselves before any heir sees it. UBS lands in the middle at $83.5 trillion, with billionaire families alone expected to hand down roughly $6.9 trillion of that by 2040. The three numbers are three different definitions of “transfer,” and the industry has spent five years quoting them as if they were one.

What the estimates do agree on is the calendar, and the calendar is the part that matters to an heir waiting on a probate court. The peak decade of transfers is 2026 to 2036, carrying approximately 55% of all transfer activity, according to Journey Advisory Group’s May 2026 analysis of Cerulli data. The first Baby Boomers turned 80 in January 2026. Approximately 2.6 million Boomers die each year today, a figure projected to approach 4 million annually by 2037. The transfer is not a single event somewhere in the future. It is a ten-year wave that has already started, and the families caught in it are the ones discovering that the waiting — not the tax, not the total — is the binding constraint.

The honest version of the argument cuts against the headline. The $124 trillion figure counts money that will never reach an heir: the $54 trillion that goes to a surviving spouse first, the decades of living expenses, the care costs, the taxes. Visa’s $36 trillion may be closer to what heirs actually receive, and if it is, the advance market is smaller than the headlines suggest — but more urgent, because the money that does move will move inside a compressed decade, through a court process that takes the better part of two years, to heirs who in most cases have no will, no plan, and no liquidity. The size of the fortune is a debate for researchers. The timing of the wait is a fact for families.

That is the implication the numbers point to, and it is the opposite of the one the headlines sell. Do not plan around $124 trillion, or $84 trillion, or $36 trillion. Plan around 2026 to 2036. That is the decade when the money moves, when the courts are busiest, when the sibling disputes peak, and when the difference between an heir who can wait twenty months and one who cannot is decided — not by the size of the inheritance, but by whether anyone arranged liquidity for the gap between the death and the distribution.

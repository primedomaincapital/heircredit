How Long Probate Takes in Your State — and What That Does to the Advance

Probate takes about 20 months on average nationally — but the same estate closes in 6 months in Texas and drags past 24 in Los Angeles County. The state sets the wait, and the wait sets the price of the advance.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

A widow in Springfield closed her late husband's estate in seven months: one house, two bank accounts, life insurance already carrying beneficiary designations. Three counties over, a family with a contested will and out-of-state property is still waiting at the two-year mark. Same state, same year, same court system — and a wait three times as long. Probate is governed by state law, and the duration of the process can range from as little as four months in Texas to more than twenty-four months in New York or California. The national average, measured from filing to final distribution, runs roughly 20 months, according to Trust & Will's 2024 State of Probate Study — and only 2 percent of the 1,000 Americans surveyed guessed it correctly. The majority guessed far shorter, or admitted they had no idea. The gap between what people expect and what the courts deliver is the whole business of the inheritance advance.

The state-by-state numbers are the first thing an heir should look up, because they set the price of every offer that follows. California runs 12 to 18 months on average, with a mandatory four-month creditor claim period and court hearing dates that take two to eight weeks to schedule; Los Angeles County backlogs run longer, and the same estate can drag past 24 months there. Texas runs 6 to 12 months, and independent administration — the most common form, available when the will permits it or all beneficiaries agree — can conclude in four to eight months; dependent administration under court supervision typically takes eight to fifteen. Florida runs 6 to 12 months for formal administration, with summary administration available for estates under $75,000 or where the decedent died more than two years ago, finishing in two to six months. New York runs 12 to 18 months on average, with Surrogate's Court backlogs in New York City pushing the range to 12 to 24 months. Illinois and Pennsylvania both run 9 to 18 months. Ohio, with a small-estate affidavit for assets under $50,000, runs 6 to 9 months.

Two statutory features do most of the work in those numbers, and they are worth understanding because they are the floor no heir can negotiate around. The first is the mandatory creditor claim period — a legally required waiting window during which creditors can file against the estate, running three to six months in most states and impossible to shortcut. It is three months in Florida and seven months in New York, a four-month swing before any estate can safely close. The second is the small-estate threshold: if the estate qualifies below it, a small estate affidavit can close it in 30 to 90 days, the single biggest time-saver available. The thresholds vary sharply — $75,000 in Texas and Florida, $50,000 in New York, Pennsylvania, and Ohio, $100,000 in Illinois — and California's is the outlier: one 2026 guide puts it at $184,500, while another source cites $208,850 as of April 1, 2025, a discrepancy that reflects a threshold adjusted over time and worth confirming with local counsel before relying on it. A simple, uncontested estate with a valid will typically closes in 6 to 12 months; estates with real property, tax filings, or multiple heirs run 12 to 24 months; and a contested will or estate litigation can add one to three years, pushing the total to three to five.

Beyond the statute, three things push an estate from months into years, and none of them appears in a state table. A contested will adds one to three years by itself, and the contest does not have to succeed to cost the time — the filing alone freezes distributions while the court sorts it out. Real property in more than one state means a second probate, called ancillary probate, in each additional state, with its own filing, its own creditor period, and its own court. And a federal estate tax return, where one is required, takes nine months or more for the tax authority to process, during which the executor cannot safely make final distributions. Court backlogs compound all of it: Los Angeles County and the New York City Surrogate's Court both carry scheduling delays that add months to an estate that would otherwise be straightforward.

What the wait does to the advance is arithmetic. Advance fees are priced off the expected timeline: three to six months at 10 to 20 percent, six to twelve months at 15 to 30 percent, twelve to twenty-four months at 25 to 40 percent, and contested cases past two years at 35 to 50 percent or more. The same $15,000 advance on the same $50,000 inheritance costs roughly $2,000 in a six-month Texas probate and roughly $5,000 in an eighteen-month California one, on the industry's published ranges. The tiered discount model makes the state difference sharper still, because the fee steps up every six months — a California estate that slips from month eleven to month thirteen crosses a pricing tier, and the heir pays for the court's calendar. And the fixed-fee model, which charges the same dollar amount regardless of duration, is worth more in a slow state than a fast one: the same $3,500 fee buys eight months of liquidity in Texas and eighteen months in California.

Here is the counterargument, and it is one the advance companies rarely make for themselves. State averages mislead, because the county matters more than the state. A fast state with a backlogged court is slower than a slow state with an efficient one: Los Angeles County runs longer than the California average, New York City runs longer than the New York average, and a rural Texas court with a light docket can close an estate faster than the Texas average suggests. The complexity tiers matter more than the geography: a simple estate in California closes faster than a contested estate in Texas, and the single biggest variable in any probate is not the state line but whether anyone contests the will, whether the estate holds real property in more than one state, and whether the executor files the inventory on time. The state table is a starting point, not a forecast — and any advance company that prices off the state average without asking which county the estate is in has not done its homework.

There is truth in that, and it cuts both ways. The county-level variation is exactly why an heir should get a case-specific estimate from a local probate attorney before signing anything, rather than relying on a national average or a funding company's quote. But the state differences are real and large enough to matter: the same estate closes in six months in Texas or drags past twenty-four in Los Angeles County, and no county-level efficiency erases a four-month creditor period in California or a seven-month one in New York. The national average of 20 months is not a target, it is a distribution — a well-organised simple estate in a fast state lands near the bottom, a contested estate with real property in a slow court lands near the top, and the heir's job is to find out which end of the distribution their estate sits on before they pay for the wait.

The implication is a sequence, not a single decision. First, find the estate's state and county, and the mandatory creditor period that sets the floor. Second, check whether the estate qualifies for a small-estate affidavit, because 30 to 90 days is a different world from 20 months. Third, ask the executor — or a local attorney — for a realistic timeline, not the statutory minimum. Fourth, and only then, price the advance against that timeline, remembering that a six-month wait and an eighteen-month wait are different products at different prices. The state sets the wait, and the wait sets the price. An heir who knows the first can negotiate the second.
